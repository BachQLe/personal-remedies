## The talking track (7 beats)

1. **Open on his assignment.** "You asked me to learn from the top planners. I went through five — Mealime, Eat This Much, PlateJoy, Samsung Food, and the ADA's Diabetes Food Hub. Here's what they actually do."

2. **Give the landscape in 30 seconds.** Four of the five hang everything on a **dated weekly calendar** — drag recipes onto Monday, Tuesday, etc. Their value is *logistics*: reducing the work of organizing meals and building a grocery run.

3. **Single out Mealime.** It's the outlier and the most-loved (4.8 stars, ~5M users). It has **no calendar at all** — you get a curated set of dinners, swipe for a new set if you don't like them, and it builds your grocery list. It kills decision fatigue. That's the interaction I want.

4. **State the distinction.** The calendar apps answer *"when will I cook this week?"* Our user doesn't have that problem — they came with a diagnosis and want the answer to *"what do I eat for my condition?"* So we borrow Mealime's simplicity and point it at conditions instead of taste. **Queues, not a calendar.**

5. **Show where we beat Mealime.** Mealime is dinner-only — no lunch, no snacks, no beverages. We have **five queues including snacks and beverages**, ranked by condition tier. That's a real gap we fill, and it's exactly the snack/beverage flagging you proposed — already built and verified in our data.

6. **Reconcile with what you said.** "The meal plan is still the vehicle — the queue *is* the plan, just without the dates. Recipes stay front and center. The daily calorie total stays. I'm not dropping the plan, I'm dropping the calendar scaffolding underneath it."

7. **Close on the one decision** (see the ask).

## Competitor cheat sheet (verified July 2026 — say these confidently)

- **Mealime** — *The model.* No calendar. Quiz (diet, allergies, dislikes, servings) → swipe through auto-suggested dinner sets → aisle-sorted grocery list. In-house recipes with photos. Calories are Pro-only ($2.99/mo) and shown per recipe, not per day. **Dinner-first — no lunch, snacks, or beverages.** Praised for killing decision fatigue; knocked for repetition once filters stack. This is the "here, eat this" simplicity we want.

- **Eat This Much** — Numbers-first. You set a calorie/macro target; it auto-fills a weekly grid to hit it, with lock-and-regenerate on individual meals. Snacks are first-class. ~$5/mo. Core value: *diet autopilot to a number.* (Borrow the **lock-and-regenerate** idea for our shuffle.)

- **PlateJoy** — Quiz-driven (~50 questions) → a designed weekly menu, no calorie counting. Snacks first-class. ~$13/mo. **Flag: its current operating status is uncertain** (pulled from Google Play in 2025) — mention only lightly, don't over-rely.

- **Samsung Food** (was Whisk) — The most calendar-heavy. Drag any saved/imported recipe onto specific days; paid tier adds AI auto-fill to a calorie goal. Free, huge community recipe pool. Core value: *universal recipe hub + shopping list.*

- **ADA Diabetes Food Hub** — Our closest medical cousin. Free, dietitian-vetted recipes you **manually drag onto a weekly calendar**; shows per-day carbs/calories/sodium. No personalization engine, no auto-fill. Core value: *trusted medical recipes, hand-planned.* (Proof that "condition-aware" sells — but it's all manual calendar, which is our opening to be smarter.)

- **"Can we actually build it?"** → "The data's already done. Three tables cached locally, 1,485 items, and snack/beverage tags now complete across the catalog. The build is mostly removing the scheduler and wiring five queues to pools we already have."
