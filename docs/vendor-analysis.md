# Meal-Plan Provider Competitive Analysis

*Prepared July 2026 for Remedi. Five vendors analyzed: Mealime, PlateJoy, eMeals, Eat This Much, and Season Health (with Foodsmart as a condition-driven comparison point). Sources cited inline; facts that could not be verified are flagged.*

---

## Executive summary

Two presentation models dominate the market:

1. **Flat recipe list** (Mealime, eMeals) — the user picks dinners from a list; there is no calendar and no meal-time slotting. Fast and low-friction, but dinner-centric: breakfast/lunch are afterthoughts (eMeals charges extra for them) and **no vendor in this model handles snacks or beverages at all**.
2. **Day view with meal slots** (Eat This Much, PlateJoy) — the day is broken into breakfast/lunch/dinner/snack sections. Eat This Much has the category's best interaction model: regenerate the whole day, shuffle a single slot, or lock a meal, with the rest adjusting automatically. CNN named it 2025's best meal-planning app.

**The condition-driven niche is wide open.** The two leaders in "meal plans for your health condition" both exited the field: PlateJoy shut down in 2025 after its 2021 acquisition by RV Health, and Season Health was absorbed into OpenLoop Health in April 2026. Neither generic planner (Mealime, Eat This Much) has any condition-specific logic beyond diet-style tags. Remedi's knowledgebase — per-food, per-condition scoring with references — is exactly the capability this market lacks.

**Pricing clusters at $2.99–$14.99/month D2C**, with annual plans at $50–$100. The condition-driven players didn't sell D2C at all — they were payer/employer-funded (B2B2C), making the product free to the end user.

## 1. Mealime

**Presentation & structure.** Not a calendar — the plan is a flat list of recipes, optionally pinned to a day, with no meal-time slotting (a recipe can't be marked "breakfast" vs "dinner"). Overwhelmingly dinner-focused; no snack or beverage handling. Plan generation is wizard-driven and fast: plan type, diet restrictions, dislikes, household size → auto-generated dinner set in under ~3 minutes. ([Plan to Eat review](https://www.plantoeat.com/blog/2023/04/mealime-app-review-pros-and-cons/), [Sunrise Digest](https://thesunrisedigest.com/eat/mealime-review-2026/))

**Interaction.** Swap individual recipes from a ~1,200-recipe library; servings only in fixed increments (2/4/6); aisle-sorted grocery list with retailer checkout; "mark as cooked" checkbox; hands-free cooking mode. Grocery list resets rather than updating incrementally when the plan changes.

**Does well:** speed-to-plan (~3 min); clean aisle-sorted grocery lists; broad diet coverage on a genuinely usable free tier; 4.8★ App Store with ~54K ratings and a multimillion install base.

**Gaps:** no week view or meal-time assignment; no nutrition tracking (static per-recipe info only); no pantry memory; recipe pool shrinks fast under filters → repetition complaints; weak desktop/web experience.

**Pricing.** Free tier is fully functional; Pro at **$2.99/mo** per the App Store listing, though mid-2026 reviews cite **$5.99/mo or $49.99/yr** (possible recent price change — sources conflict). Pro adds full nutrition data, calorie filtering, past plans, exclusive recipes. ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/mealime-meal-plans-recipes/id1079999103), [support docs](https://support.mealime.com/article/79-mealime-pro))

**Reach.** App-store-led organic growth + content/SEO blog + a B2B arm ("Mealime for Work") selling corporate wellness bundles to employers with an ROI pitch. ([work.mealime.com](https://work.mealime.com/for-employers))

## 2. PlateJoy — *shut down 2025; cautionary tale*

**Presentation & structure.** Weekly menu with **breakfast/lunch/dinner recipe slots** (one recipe per meal per day), generated from a 50+ data-point quiz (health goals, 14+ diet types incl. diabetic-friendly/low-FODMAP/low-sodium, allergies, even available kitchen appliances). Snack/beverage slot handling unverified. ([Garage Gym Reviews](https://www.garagegymreviews.com/platejoy-review))

**Interaction.** Signature **Swap** button per recipe — alternates chosen to minimize food waste by reusing ingredients already on the grocery list; goal-linked portion sizing; grocery list with pantry-item suppression and Instacart/Amazon Fresh ordering. ([support.platejoy.com](https://support.platejoy.com/en/articles/1038159-what-happens-when-i-click-swap-on-my-menu))

**Did well:** deep personalization; waste-aware swaps; real clinical credibility — a CDC-recognized Diabetes Prevention Program with >20,000 prediabetic users and a cited 60% reduction in T2D risk.

**Gaps / why it died:** finite recipe pool that froze after the 2021 RV Health acquisition ("really really boring" — Trustpilot); billing/support collapse (users charged after cancellation, support unreachable); app pulled from stores by March 2025. The product concept was never the problem — post-acquisition neglect was.

**Pricing (historical).** $12.99/mo, $69/6mo, $99/yr D2C. The Diabetes Prevention Program was **free to end users when covered by insurers/employers** (BCBS plans, Express Scripts, Dignity Health, Kroger — ~20M eligible lives), including a free connected scale and Fitbit. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/02/22/platejoy-is-introducing-a-digital-health-system-to-prevent-diabetes.html))

**Reach.** The standout: **B2B2C health-plan distribution** — selling a clinically measurable program to insurers who gave it to members free, a near-zero-CAC acquisition channel. Also served DoD/Air Force. ([Quartz](https://qz.com/1225685/platejoy-got-medical-insurance-to-cover-the-cost-of-the-meal-plans-it-sells))

## 3. eMeals

**Presentation & structure.** Weekly **dietitian-curated menus** across ~15 diet-style plans — including **Diabetic (30–55g carbs/meal) and Heart Healthy (~800mg sodium/meal)**, the closest any mainstream player gets to condition-driven planning. Dinner is the core product (7 new recipes/week); **breakfast and lunch are paid add-ons**; snacks absent. Users pick-and-choose from the week's plan rather than following it rigidly. Mobile-app-only viewing — a real desktop gap. ([Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/emeals-review), [deliveryrank.com](https://www.deliveryrank.com/reviews/emeals))

**Interaction.** Pick a diet plan → select recipes → auto-generated, department-sorted shopping list → one-tap handoff to **Walmart, Kroger brands, Instacart, Amazon Fresh, Albertsons/Safeway**. No allergen/preference filtering inside a plan.

**Does well:** kills "what's for dinner" decision fatigue; deepest grocery-fulfillment integration in the category; remarkable retention (reviewers citing 10–15 years of use); quantified condition targets per plan.

**Gaps:** no allergen filtering; mobile-only; portion-size mismatches; **severe cancellation/billing complaints** (phone-only cancellation, surprise renewals, no-refund policy — recurring across Trustpilot/BBB/PissedConsumer).

**Pricing.** Dinner: $37.50/3mo or **$59.99/yr**; breakfast add-on $29.99/yr; lunch add-on $39.99/yr; All-Day bundle $99.98/yr; 14-day trial. No monthly-only option — commitment terms only. ([support.emeals.com](https://support.emeals.com/portal/en/kb/articles/emeals-cost))

**Reach.** Affiliate program (Commission Junction); **BrandBuilder** — CPG brands (Tyson, Campbell's, General Mills) pay for shoppable placement inside recipes and lists; **Grocery Connect** white-label SDK licensed to retailers; a Health + Wellness B2B channel selling bulk subscriptions to insurers/employers; dietitian-bylined content marketing.

## 4. Eat This Much

**Presentation & structure.** The purest **auto-generator**: enter calorie target, macros, meal count, diet style → a full day (free) or week (Premium) generated in seconds, organized into **configurable meal slots (up to 9/day)** — breakfast/lunch/dinner/snacks by default, renameable ("Pre-Workout Snack"), drag-to-reorder. No dedicated beverage slot (beverages are just food items). CNN's "Best Meal Planning App" 2025. ([eatthismuch.com](https://www.eatthismuch.com/), [help docs](https://help.eatthismuch.com/help/changing-the-number-of-meals))

**Interaction — the category's gold standard:**
- **Regenerate** the whole day with one button;
- **Shuffle** a single meal slot without touching the rest;
- **Lock** a meal ("I ate this") so regenerations skip it;
- **Auto-rebalance**: swap one meal and the others adjust to still hit daily targets;
- automatic leftovers scheduling; virtual pantry; Instacart/AmazonFresh checkout.

**Does well:** instant generation; fine-grained control without starting over; polished apps (4.6–4.7★, 32K+ ratings); real grocery integration.

**Gaps:** recipe repetition is the #1 complaint ("hundreds of times" across stores/Reddit — worsens by week 3–4); small pool (~5K recipes) shrinks under restrictions; recipe-quality complaints; grocery-list quantity errors; one review flagged an unsafely low ~894 kcal/day recommendation with no guardrails; restrictive free tier.

**Pricing.** Free: daily generation + tracking. Premium: **$5/mo billed annually (~$60/yr) or $14.99/mo**, 14-day trial, 30-day money-back. A **Professional tier** (custom pricing) serves dietitians/coaches with multi-client dashboards and white-labeling. ([eatthismuch.com/pricing](https://www.eatthismuch.com/pricing))

**Reach.** Content/SEO-led: a free public **calorie calculator** captures long-tail search traffic; tutorial blog; strong app-store presence; earned media (CNN); heavy organic presence in diet-app comparison content.

## 5. Season Health (+ Foodsmart) — *acquired April 2026*

**Presentation & structure.** "Food as medicine," **dietitian-mediated** rather than self-serve: quiz → insurance check → matched registered-dietitian video visit → personalized condition plan (diabetes, heart disease, CKD) → recipes and pre-made meals shopped from an integrated marketplace (Instacart, Walmart, Daily Harvest, Albertsons). No visible day/week slot UI. Foodsmart, the closest self-serve analog, runs NutriQuiz → **swappable weekly plan** → grocery list pushed to Amazon Fresh/Instacart, with condition programs like "Heart-Smart."

**Interaction.** Season: the dietitian relationship is the product; eating the plan = shopping the plan-compliant marketplace. Foodsmart: self-serve plan with swaps, independent of visits. Both are pass-through to third-party grocery delivery — no owned logistics.

**Does well:** clinical credibility (Season cites an HbA1c reduction of 1.6); insurance-covered visits (free for ~95% of patients); **per-meal "why this fits your condition" reasoning** — the one feature nobody else has.

**Gaps:** thin self-serve UI; food itself still out-of-pocket; value gated behind a live dietitian; Foodsmart supports only "top 8" allergens with no custom exclusions; and Season no longer exists independently — acquired by OpenLoop Health (April 2026), signaling that standalone food-as-medicine struggles to scale without a distribution host.

**Pricing & model.** B2B2C, payer/employer-funded — not D2C. Season visits free in-network for ~95%; a ~$75/mo concierge figure is referenced but not fully verified. Foodsmart: $0 gated per-payer (Cigna, Medicaid plans, Aetna Medicare, Mass General Brigham).

**Reach.** Almost entirely payer/employer partnerships and provider referrals; essentially no consumer marketing. The OpenLoop acquisition itself was a distribution play.

## What Remedi should take from this

### Features to adopt (ranked)
1. **Slot-based day view with regenerate / shuffle / check-off** (Eat This Much) — the proven interaction model; now being implemented on Remedi's Plan screen.
2. **Per-item "why this fits your condition"** (Season Health) — Remedi's API can back this with real tier + reference data, at self-serve scale, which Season could only do through a human dietitian.
3. **Quantified condition targets made legible** (eMeals) — "30–55g carbs per meal" style framing turns clinical value into marketing copy.
4. **Waste-aware swap logic** (PlateJoy) — small mechanic, outsized user love.
5. **Grocery-list handoff to retailers** (all five) — table stakes for a later phase; affiliate pass-through, never owned logistics.
6. **Speed-to-first-plan under 3 minutes** (Mealime) — the onboarding bar to beat.

### Gaps every vendor shares (Remedi's openings)
- **Nobody handles snacks and beverages as first-class plan slots.** Remedi's API has dedicated scored food groups for both (h1, h2) — Mory's requirement is also a genuine differentiator.
- **No mainstream planner has per-condition food scoring.** Diet-style tags ("diabetic-friendly") are the ceiling; the two companies that went deeper are gone.
- **Trust hygiene**: billing/cancellation abuse is the most consistent complaint across eMeals, PlateJoy, and others — transparent self-service cancellation is a cheap differentiator.
- **Repetition**: every generator gets accused of it; honest "shuffle deeper into the scored list" beats fake variety.

### How they reach prospects — channel summary
- **App-store organic + ratings flywheel**: Mealime, Eat This Much
- **Content/SEO with a free tool as bait**: Eat This Much (calorie calculator) — Remedi's analog could be a per-condition "foods to avoid" checker
- **Affiliate/comparison-site placement**: eMeals, Eat This Much
- **CPG brand placement inside recipes**: eMeals BrandBuilder
- **Employer wellness**: Mealime for Work, eMeals H+W
- **Payer/insurer B2B2C (free to end user)**: PlateJoy DPP, Season, Foodsmart — the highest-leverage channel for a condition-driven product, and the one Remedi's clinical framing is best positioned for long-term.
